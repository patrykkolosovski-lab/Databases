# Academic pressure and non-medical use of study drugs

## 1. Newspaper article and source

Our selected newspaper article is Rachel Goldsmith's "Campus researchers address student usage of 'study drugs' and potential consequences", published on 19 April 2026 in Pipe Dream. Pipe Dream is Binghamton University's independent student newspaper, founded in 1946, with a print edition and an editorial newsroom; this is a reported news article rather than a personal blog. Its campus focus makes it directly relevant to students and university support services.

## 2. The societal challenge in our own words

Our challenge is non-medical use of prescription medicines for studying under academic pressure. Students may take these medicines without a prescription or use them differently from their prescribed treatment because they expect greater concentration or productivity. This raises questions about student wellbeing, expectations of performance and access to support. Medically indicated treatment must be distinguished from misuse: receiving an ADHD prescription does not establish inappropriate use.

The news article describes campus experiences and interviews with researchers and counselling staff. Its informal survey cannot establish how common misuse is among all students. We therefore use scientific research to assess the wider evidence rather than treating a campus news story as a representative prevalence study.

## 3. Why we are interested

As students, we experience academic pressure and recognise the temptation to seek shortcuts when deadlines accumulate. We are concerned both about peers' wellbeing and about the belief that taking medication is necessary to keep up. We want to understand the evidence behind these beliefs and explore how structured information could support better questions about prescription patterns. Our own experiences motivate the project; they are not evidence of how widespread misuse is.

## 4. Stakeholders

- Students: need reliable information, appropriate treatment and accessible support without stigma.
- University staff and counselling services: can use evidence to develop wellbeing support and address academic pressure.
- Doctors and other healthcare providers: need to distinguish appropriate treatment from non-medical use and support patients responsibly.
- Parents and families: may support students experiencing stress or medication-related difficulties.
- People receiving prescribed ADHD treatment: could be harmed if legitimate treatment is confused with misuse.
- Employers: have an interest in how expectations of constant productivity extend beyond university.

## 5. Four complementary scientific articles

### Paper 1: Prevalence and quality of evidence

Jones and Newton (2024) review surveys of cognitive-enhancer use among UK university students. Subtype: peer-reviewed brief report synthesising survey evidence. Their analysis shows why sampling matters: the included studies did not use representative sampling, and evidence of an increase over time was inconclusive. This helps us avoid generalising campus anecdotes to all students. It also shows that self-reported use and prescription records answer different questions. Its UK focus limits direct transfer to other countries.

### Paper 2: Benefits, harms and uncertainty

Schifano et al. (2022) examine cognitive enhancers in healthy individuals. Subtype: peer-reviewed review article. They discuss uncertain benefits and possible cardiovascular, neurological and psychological complications. The review helps explain why perceived improvements should not be treated as proof of safe or effective academic enhancement. It also supports distinguishing non-medical use in healthy individuals from clinically indicated treatment. Because it covers several substances and heterogeneous evidence, it does not establish that every drug has identical effects or that every user experiences harm.

### Paper 3: Access through informal markets

Demant et al. (2020) study prescription-drug distribution through observations of Swedish Facebook drug markets. Subtype: peer-reviewed original qualitative research. This adds a social perspective: access to medicines can extend beyond formal prescribing. It helps explain why a prescription database alone cannot measure all non-medical use. Its findings concern a particular online market and should not be treated as a prevalence estimate for university students or as a description of every social-media platform.

### Paper 4: Testing claims of cognitive enhancement

Ilieva et al. (2015) analyse 48 studies involving 1,409 participants to assess stimulant effects on selected cognitive functions in healthy people. Subtype: peer-reviewed meta-analysis. They find benefits on some tasks but conclude that overall enhancement is probably modest, with publication-bias concerns affecting some findings. This complements the broader review by directly assessing the magnitude of cognitive effects. Laboratory task improvements do not automatically establish better grades, long-term benefits or safety. The paper helps us frame academic-enhancement claims cautiously.

## 6. Synthesis and implications for our database

Together, these papers address prevalence, potential harms, informal access and cognitive effects. They support a focused project on prescription patterns while showing its limits. Our database records students, doctors, medicines and prescriptions, allowing questions about prescription history, frequency and differences between programmes. It does not currently record diagnoses, actual consumption, non-prescribed purchases, motivation, academic pressure or outcomes. Consequently, it cannot identify misuse, evaluate clinical appropriateness or establish that medication causes academic success or harm. Those questions would require additional suitable data and careful study design.
